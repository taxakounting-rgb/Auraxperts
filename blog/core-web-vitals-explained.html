Core Web Vitals Explained: Why Website Speed Affects Your Sales

AuraXperts Team 7 Oct 2025 4 min read
Share

Google measures how fast and stable your website feels to real users. These measurements influence rankings — and, more importantly, whether visitors stay and buy.

You've probably experienced it: you tap a link, stare at a blank screen, and give up before the page loads. Or you go to tap a button and the page suddenly jumps, so you hit an advert instead. These everyday frustrations are exactly what Google's Core Web Vitals are designed to measure.

Core Web Vitals are a set of metrics that describe how a real person experiences your website — how quickly it loads, how quickly it responds, and how stable it is while loading. They matter for search rankings, but their bigger impact is on your conversions. A faster, smoother website keeps more visitors, and more visitors means more enquiries and sales.

Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): how fast it loads

LCP measures how long it takes for the largest visible element on the screen — usually a hero image, a large heading or a video — to appear. In plain terms, it's how long a visitor waits before the page looks ready. Google considers an LCP of 2.5 seconds or less to be good.

Slow LCP is usually caused by large, uncompressed images, slow servers, or heavy code that has to load before the main content can be displayed. Common fixes include compressing and resizing images, serving them in modern formats like WebP or AVIF, using a content delivery network, and making sure the hero image is prioritised rather than loaded late.

Interaction to Next Paint (INP): how fast it responds

INP measures how quickly your page reacts when someone interacts with it — tapping a button, opening a menu, typing in a form. If there's a noticeable delay between the tap and the visual response, the site feels sluggish and broken. Google considers an INP of 200 milliseconds or less to be good. INP replaced an older metric, First Input Delay, in 2024 because it captures responsiveness across the whole visit, not just the first interaction.

Poor INP is usually caused by too much JavaScript running in the browser. Heavy page builders, many third-party scripts (chat widgets, trackers, pop-ups) and inefficient code all compete for the browser's attention. Removing scripts you don't need, deferring non-essential ones, and simplifying interactive elements make a big difference.

Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): how stable it is

CLS measures how much the page jumps around while it loads. You've seen this when text moves down because an image or advert suddenly appears above it. It's not just annoying; it causes people to tap the wrong thing. Google considers a CLS score of 0.1 or less to be good.

The main causes are images and embeds without defined dimensions, ads or banners injected above existing content, and web fonts that swap in late and change the size of text. Setting width and height on images, reserving space for dynamic content, and loading fonts carefully keep the layout steady.

Why this affects your bottom line

Speed and stability shape a visitor's first impression of your business. A slow, jumpy website feels unprofessional and untrustworthy, even if your products are excellent. Visitors on mobile data are especially impatient; every extra second of waiting increases the chance they'll leave and choose a competitor.

There's also a ranking benefit. Google uses page experience signals, including Core Web Vitals, as part of how it ranks pages. Great content still matters most, but when two pages are similarly relevant, the one that offers the better experience has an edge.

How to check your scores

Google PageSpeed Insights is the easiest place to start. Enter your URL and you'll see both real-user data (if your site has enough traffic) and a lab test with specific recommendations. Google Search Console also has a Core Web Vitals report that groups your pages into "good," "needs improvement" and "poor," so you can see where to focus.

Always check mobile results first. Most of your visitors are on phones, and mobile scores are typically worse than desktop.

Where to begin

For most business websites, the biggest wins come from optimising images, reducing unnecessary plugins and third-party scripts, and choosing quality hosting. These changes alone often move a site from "poor" to "good." More advanced improvements — code splitting, caching strategies, server-side rendering — can follow.

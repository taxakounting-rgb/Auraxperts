7 UX Design Mistakes That Quietly Kill Your Conversions

AuraXperts Team 13 Jan 2026 4 min read
Share

Visitors rarely complain about a confusing website — they just leave. These common user-experience mistakes cost businesses customers every day without anyone noticing.

User experience problems are sneaky. Unlike a broken link or an error message, they don't announce themselves. Visitors who get confused or frustrated simply leave, and you never hear about it. Your analytics show a high bounce rate or a low conversion rate, but not why. Meanwhile, competitors with clearer websites quietly win the customers you were paying to attract.

Here are seven of the most common UX mistakes we see on business websites, why they hurt, and how to fix them.

1. Navigation that reflects your org chart, not your customers

Many websites organise their menus around how the business sees itself — departments, internal service names, industry jargon. Customers don't think that way. They think in terms of their problems and goals. If a visitor can't quickly find the page that matches what they need, they'll assume you don't offer it.

Keep your main navigation short, use plain words your customers would use, and put the most important pages first. Test it by asking someone outside your business to find specific information on your site and watching where they get stuck.

2. Walls of text with no visual hierarchy

People don't read web pages word by word; they scan. When a page is a dense block of paragraphs with no clear headings, scanning becomes impossible, and visitors give up. Good visual hierarchy guides the eye: a clear headline, short paragraphs, descriptive subheadings, and enough white space for content to breathe.

This doesn't mean dumbing content down. Detailed information is valuable — it just needs structure so people can find the parts that matter to them.

3. Too many choices at once

When a page offers ten equally prominent buttons, five pop-ups and three different offers, visitors experience decision paralysis. With no obvious path forward, many choose nothing at all. Each page should have one primary action that stands out visually, with secondary options clearly less prominent.

4. Forms that feel like an interrogation

Long forms with unclear labels, unnecessary required fields and unhelpful error messages are conversion killers. Ask only for what you genuinely need at this stage, label every field clearly, show helpful examples, and if something goes wrong, explain exactly how to fix it — in plain language, right next to the field.

On mobile, use the right input types so the correct keyboard appears automatically: a number pad for phone numbers, an email keyboard for email addresses. Small details like this remove friction at the exact moment someone is trying to contact you.

5. Designing for desktop and hoping mobile works

Most visitors now browse on phones, yet many sites are still designed on large monitors and squeezed onto small screens as an afterthought. The result is tiny text, buttons too close together to tap accurately, and menus that are hard to use with a thumb.

Design mobile-first: make tap targets large, keep important actions within easy reach, and test every key journey — finding a service, reading about it, contacting you — on an actual phone.

6. Low contrast and hard-to-read text

Light grey text on a white background might look elegant in a design mockup, but in real life — on a phone, outdoors, in sunlight — it's hard to read. Poor contrast affects everyone, and it particularly excludes people with visual impairments. Use strong contrast between text and background, comfortable font sizes, and adequate line spacing.

7. Hiding the information people need to decide

Visitors often arrive with specific questions: How much does it cost? How long does it take? Do you work in my area? What happens after I contact you? If they can't find answers, they leave to look elsewhere. You don't always need to publish exact prices, but you should address these questions honestly — with starting prices, typical timelines, a clear explanation of your process, or a helpful FAQ page.

How to find your own UX problems

The most revealing exercise is watching real people use your website. Ask a few people who match your customer profile to complete specific tasks while thinking aloud, and resist the urge to help. You'll quickly see where they hesitate, misunderstand or get lost. Tools like heatmaps and session recordings can supplement this by showing how visitors behave at scale.
